Emergency grab bag checklist

Last updated October 2026

If you're told to evacuate because of flooding, fire or a gas leak, you may have only minutes to leave. A grab bag is a small, ready-packed bag kept near the door, holding what you'd need for the first day or two away from home.

Keep it light enough for the smallest adult in the house to carry, tell everyone where it is, and check it every six months: swap the water and snacks, recharge the power bank and update medication.

What to pack

  1. Grab bag

    A ready-packed bag in case you need to leave home quickly.

    For one person: 1 per household
    For a family of four: 1 per household

    If you're asked to evacuate because of flooding or fire, you may have minutes to leave. A packed bag by the door holds your essentials, documents and medication.

    • A waterproof rucksack of 30 to 40 litres
    • Easy to carry for the smallest adult in the house
    • Pack a torch, water, snacks, medication, chargers and copies of documents

  3. Pill organiser & medicine bag

    Keep several days of essential medication sorted and ready to go. On the government's list.

    For one person: 1 per person on regular medication
    For a family of four: 1 per person on regular medication

    The government advises having enough of your regular medication to last several days, in case pharmacies are closed or you can't travel. An organiser makes it easy to grab and go.

    • A weekly pill organiser with clearly marked days
    • A small insulated bag if any medicine needs to stay cool
    • Keep a paper copy of every prescription in the bag

  4. First aid kit

    Plasters, bandages, dressings, antiseptic and the basics to treat minor injuries. On the government's list.

    For one person: 1 home kit
    For a family of four: 1 home kit

    When ambulances and GP surgeries are stretched you may need to deal with minor injuries yourself. The government suggests plasters, bandages, a thermometer, antiseptic, eyewash, sterile dressings, gloves, medical tape and tweezers.

    • A kit that meets British Standard BS 8599-1 for home or workplace use
    • A digital thermometer if the kit doesn't include one
    • A first aid guide booklet, in case the internet is down

  5. Wind-up or battery torch

    Light that works when the mains is off. Far safer than candles. On the government's list.

    For one person: 1 torch, one per adult, or 1 head torch 2-pack
    For a family of four: 2 torches, one per adult, or 1 head torch 2-pack

    The government's Prepare campaign lists a battery or wind-up torch first, because candles are a leading cause of house fires during power cuts. A wind-up model never runs out of batteries.

    • A hand-crank or USB-rechargeable LED torch, ideally with both
    • At least 100 lumens for moving around the house
    • A head torch as well, so your hands are free

  6. Portable power bank

    Keeps phones charged so you can get alerts and call for help. On the government's list.

    For one person: 1 power bank of 20,000 mAh
    For a family of four: 1 power bank of 20,000 mAh

    Your phone receives the government's Emergency Alerts and is how you'll contact family. A charged power bank gives several full phone charges during a long outage.

    • 20,000 mAh or more for several phone charges
    • The right cables for every phone in the house
    • Keep it topped up: check and recharge every three months
    • A car charger lets you charge phones from the car during a power cut

  7. Bottled water

    The single most important thing in your kit. On the government's list.

    For one person: 9–30 litres for 3 days: 1–4 packs of 6 × 1.5L
    For a family of four: 36–120 litres for 3 days: 4–14 packs of 6 × 1.5L

    The government recommends 2.5 to 3 litres per person per day just to drink, or 10 litres per person per day to cover cooking and washing. Plan for at least three days.

    • Sealed multipacks of still water, the cheapest and safest option
    • Store in a cool, dark place, off the floor
    • Use and replace it before the best-before date

  8. Emergency food rations

    Compact, long shelf life bars that you can forget about for years.

  9. Foil emergency blankets

    Light, cheap and they keep in body heat during a winter power cut.

    For one person: 2 blankets
    For a family of four: 4 blankets

    Without power most central heating stops. Foil blankets weigh almost nothing and help keep people warm, especially older people and young children who feel the cold faster.

    • A pack of several blankets, at least 130 x 210 cm
    • Keep warm layers and normal blankets with your kit too
    • Put one or two in the car for winter

  10. Emergency whistle

    Signal for help if you're trapped or separated. Louder than shouting and needs no power.

    For one person: 1 whistle, one per person (1 × 3-pack)
    For a family of four: 4 whistles, one per person (2 × 3-pack)

    A whistle carries much further than your voice and takes little effort, which matters if you're injured or trapped. Keep one in the grab bag and one on each person's keys or coat.

    • A pealess design, so it still works when wet
    • Bright orange, so it's easy to find in a bag

  11. Hand sanitiser & wet wipes

    Stay clean and healthy when the taps don't work. On the government's list.

    For one person: 1 bottle of sanitiser (1 pack of 4) and 2 packs of wipes (1 box of 18)
    For a family of four: 2 bottles of sanitiser (1 pack of 4) and 4 packs of wipes (1 box of 18)

    If your water supply is cut off, hand sanitiser and wet wipes are the government's recommended way to keep hands and surfaces clean and stop illness spreading.

    • Sanitiser with at least 60% alcohol
    • Large packs of unscented wet wipes

  13. Basic backup mobile phone

    Weeks of battery on one charge, for calls and texts when your smartphone is flat.

    For one person: 1 per household
    For a family of four: 1 per household

    A simple phone such as a Nokia 105 lasts weeks on standby, far longer than a smartphone. Put a pay-as-you-go SIM in it: in the UK a phone needs a SIM to call 999, but emergency calls then work on any network with signal, even with no credit.

Frequently asked questions

What should be in a grab bag?

Copies of important documents, medication, a first aid kit, a torch, a charged power bank and cable, water, snacks, a foil blanket, a whistle, hand sanitiser and a little cash. Add essentials for babies, children and pets.

Where should I keep my grab bag?

Somewhere near the main door that everyone knows about, not in the loft or a cellar. In a flood-risk area, keep it upstairs if possible.

How often should I check my grab bag?

Every six months. Replace water and food before their dates, recharge power banks, test the torch and update any medication and documents.
